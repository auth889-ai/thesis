Who sees the jam first?

Reproducing the world's best road-traffic forecasters on one protocol, scoring them at the moment a jam forms, and turning the result into advice a person can use.

1 · The problem

A person asks "when do I leave"; the city answers with an average.

Every traffic forecasting paper reports one number: mean error over every five-minute window of a test month. Most of those windows are free flow, where repeating the last reading is already good. The windows that decide whether someone is late are the few where a road tips from 60 mph to 20.

Free flow: above 0.6 × the road's own free-flow speed (85th percentile of its training-split readings). The same rule throughout.

2 · The gap

Error triples at the transition, and the field does not report it.

A 2026 study on three time-series foundation models found transition-regime error of 11 mph against 3 overall, and 90 % prediction intervals covering about half the outcomes there. At the same time, foundation models now match purpose-trained graph networks on the aggregate number without ever seeing the city.

Baseline papers: Yanes-Pulido & Rodrigues 2026, arXiv 2602.24238; the regime study, arXiv 2606.18367 (ICML 2026 workshop).

Two claims, one unanswered question. Foundation models are "strong baselines in transportation". Foundation models "hide regime-dependent failures". Nobody has put the field's trained models and the foundation models on one table at the moment that matters.
3 · The question

Across the best trained and zero-shot models, who detects congestion onset, how early, with honest intervals?

Two jobs, two protocols. Forecasting: speed at +15, +30, +60 minutes. Onset detection: the first congested window per road, its lead time, and whether the interval around the forecast was honest at that moment. Protocol A is the papers' own split. Protocol B scores free flow, transition and congestion separately. They are never averaged.

8 · The intervals, the method chapter

A "90 %" interval reported the usual way covers a third of jam transitions.

MAPIE split-conformal intervals on each model's point forecast, calibrated on a validation block and scored on the test block. Three calibrators: one global quantile; one per regime observed now; one per regime the model itself predicts at the horizon.

● global   ● regime observed now   ● regime the model predicts. Coverage at 15 minutes on the windows where free flow turns into congestion, all 207 roads, mean over three seeds with the spread on the last point. Overall coverage of every row is 0.90; in congestion the predicted-regime calibrator reaches 0.86 to 0.87.

9 · What the method can and cannot fix

Conditioning on the regime the model predicts repairs most of congestion and recovers a fifth of the transition gap.

Conditioning on the regime observed now makes transitions worse, because at a transition the observed regime is still free flow. Conditioning on the model's own forecast lifts MegaCRN from 45 % to 52 % at 15 minutes over three seeds, and no calibrator reaches 90 % there. The transition is a forecasting problem, not a calibration problem: an interval can only be honest about a jam the model has some signal of.

10 · A city with few sensors

Dhaka has no loop detectors. Two answers: the phone, and imputation.

The app turns a phone into a sensor: shared fixes are snapped to the road with Amazon Location, binned into 70-metre road-direction cells every five minutes, device ids hashed, raw fixes purged after a day. On the benchmark side, hiding a quarter of METR-LA's observed points and filling them back under the ImputeFormer paper's protocol validates the setup exactly, mean-fill 7.51 against the paper's 7.52, and shows the learned imputer overtaking linear interpolation after 60 laptop epochs, with the paper's full budget still ahead.

12 · Live demo, three minutes

Watch a jam form, and get told when to leave.

  1. Open the city map on Los Angeles at a morning moment. Pick MegaCRN, then STAEformer, then Chronos-2: the forecast changes, the checkpoint hash sits in the corner.
  2. Scroll to "About to tip": the roads the model forecasts into congestion within the hour. Watch one.
  3. Open "Leave when?": a corridor, the forecast per leg, the 90 % worst case, the latest departure for a deadline.
  4. Open the Onset tab: the scorecard behind the advice, and the interval coverage that says how honest it is.
  5. On a phone, press "Share my trip" on the Map page and watch a cell appear.
